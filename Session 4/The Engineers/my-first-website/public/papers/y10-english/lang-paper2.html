Read the two sources carefully before answering the questions.

Source A: 21st Century non-fiction article
Title: "Should Social Media Be Banned for Under-16s?"
The debate over social media use among teenagers has reached fever pitch. Last week, the Health Secretary announced plans to restrict under-16s from accessing platforms like Instagram and TikTok. Supporters claim this will protect young minds from harmful content and cyberbullying. Critics argue it infringes on free expression and ignores the educational benefits of digital connectivity.

As a teacher of fifteen years, I've witnessed both sides. Social media connects isolated students and enables collaborative learning. Yet I've also seen self-esteem crumble under the weight of filtered perfection and anonymous cruelty. The solution isn't blanket bans—it's education, parental involvement, and holding platforms accountable for their algorithms.

We wouldn't ban cars because some people drive dangerously; we teach road safety. Similarly, we must teach digital literacy while demanding safer online spaces.
Source B: 19th Century non-fiction letter
Title: Extract from a letter (1894) about the dangers of penny dreadfuls
My dear Margaret,

I write with grave concern regarding these so-called 'penny dreadfuls' that our youth consume with such voracious appetite. These sensational tales of crime and adventure, priced for the masses, are corrupting the minds of impressionable boys.

The police report that several recent thefts were perpetrated by lads who claimed inspiration from such literature. The descriptions of violence are most graphic, and the heroes are invariably criminals! What lessons do such books teach our children—that robbery is adventurous? That murder can be justified?

I urge you to inspect your son's reading matter. These publications must be banned from respectable homes. Let us return to the improving literature of previous generations, which taught duty, honour, and Christian values.
